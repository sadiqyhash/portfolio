As a Lie, As a Dream

2025 — Ongoing

Photography and text

Text

Every day, I yearn for the normal life we once had in Yemen a decade ago, before the conflict engulfed everything. The dictionary defines “normal” as something familiar and regularly encountered, but what constitutes a normal life? What is the normal?

I once dreamt of a simple existence, attending school, acquiring knowledge, and forging a future for myself. Perhaps I would even work at an engineering firm or office someday. However, over the past ten years, my life underwent a profound transformation. My aspirations shifted, and even my path altered. I found myself in an entirely different field, striving to make something of myself, determined to demonstrate that war cannot be the ultimate end to our dreams.

In Yemen, these steps (studying, working, marrying, and raising a family) are the anticipated milestones of a “normal” life. So much so that simply dreaming, especially deviating from conventional norms, becomes an anomaly. Perhaps that’s what defines a “normal life”—the repetition of certain routines by a sufficient number of individuals until they become the accepted script, the agreed-upon path. Anything that dares to deviate from this path becomes “abnormal.”

When contemplating all this, my mind frequently wanders to Jonathan Reed’s poem, Lost Generation. It commences with the line:

“I am a part of a lost generation
And I refuse to believe
that I can change the world…”

The poem begins with a sombre outlook, mirroring the pessimism passed down through generations—the weight of their expectations, their perception of the world, and their demands that we rectify it. It’s a heavy, imposed narrative.

However, halfway through, Reed introduces a profound shift. He invites us to read the poem in reverse—from bottom to top—and suddenly, everything transforms. The meaning is reborn.

And those very lines take on a new significance:

“I can change the world
And I refuse to believe
that I am part of a lost generation.”

And just like that, the power returns to the dreamer—the ability to define for himself what a normal life should look like. He believes that, even here, even now, something else is possible.
