Sadiq Al-Harasi is a Yemeni visual artist and curator based in Denmark. Holding a degree in Architectural Engineering, Sadiq approaches his artistic practice with a structural sensitivity toward space and site, examining how personal and collective identities are reconstructed in the aftermath of loss.

His work investigates the tension between silenced histories and dominant narratives through archival research, oral history, and documentary photography. By navigating the intersections of displacement, grief, and inherited memory, Sadiq interrogates how individuals and communities anchor themselves within fragmented realities. Working across analogue and digital photography, sound, and installations, he develops a multidisciplinary storytelling practice that reconsiders the spatial and psychological construction of home and memory.

His work has been exhibited internationally, with presentations including Slidefest SAFAR (Peckham 24), Image Festival Amman (2025), LagosPhoto Biennale (2025), VAiF (2025), and Loss Marginalia: Elegiac Whispers.

Artist statement

My research-based practice examines how personal archives shape memory, identity, and belonging across generations. Through these archives, I explore how dominant narratives obscure lived experience within societies shaped by war, displacement, and cultural erasure.

Working across traditional and experimental media, I allow each project to determine its own form. Photography, moving image, sound, cyanotype, text, and installation are methods of inquiry rather than fixed disciplines. I assemble intergenerational memory that transforms personal experience into a site of collective reflection. The human form becomes a recurring point of departure through which I ask: How does memory function in the aftermath of loss? Can personal recollections become resistant forms of history?

Each project begins with research, drawing on family albums, damaged photographs, handwritten notebooks, official records, domestic objects, poems, oral testimonies, and recordings of my mother's songs. Rather than treating these materials as historical evidence, I approach them as living fragments through which memory is continually negotiated and reconstructed. Through my work, I bring these fragments into dialogue, inviting viewers to encounter the gaps, silences, and contradictions that shape both personal and collective histories.

My visual language reflects the fragmented nature of memory through layering, monochrome photography, spatial interventions, movement, and material assemblage. Rather than presenting fixed narratives, my works remain open structures that invite viewers to bring their own memories into conversation with mine, creating spaces of shared interpretation.

Growing up in Yemen has profoundly shaped my understanding of grief, absence, resilience, and self-discovery. Creating as a self-taught artist in a context where art is often dismissed as a luxury has strengthened my commitment to storytelling and cultural preservation. Working amid scarcity and constraint has reinforced the urgency of creating spaces where Yemeni narratives can participate in broader conversations about memory, resilience, and cultural continuity.

Rather than preserving fixed histories, I seek to create evolving archives where photographs, oral histories, everyday objects, and family records continue to generate new meanings. By weaving together personal and collective narratives, my work asks how archives can remain living structures through which memory continues to be shared, questioned, and reimagined.

Because memory is layered, fragile, and often fragmented, a single medium is rarely enough to capture its weight.
