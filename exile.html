In Exile, You Have No Memory

Date 2026

Format / Medium Documentary photography

Text

Beyond one of Europe’s most structured asylum systems, where people seek safety and a more sustainable future, lies a parallel, elastic world of time. It does not move forward. Instead, it sways in shared rooms, in corridors heavy with the scent of muted anxiety, and in train stations transformed into involuntary landmarks of encounters and farewells. In exile, memory loses its linear trajectory. The geography of home morphs into an archive of scents and absence, while the geography of the asylum camp becomes a precise architecture of what its inhabitants call a psychological prison.

The high-speed rail lines slice through the flat, manicured Dutch landscape, carrying passengers who cross borders in minutes. For Suhail and Sama, a married Yemeni asylum-seeking couple in their thirties, the two-hour train journey between Zutphen’s eastern municipality and The Hague’s political urban area has become a chasm. Their geography is no longer defined by borders but by asylum appointments, train schedules, and reception centres, which have increasingly left them stranded in legal and emotional limbo.

Tracing the human threads that bind Suhail and Sama reveals how deeply personal histories intersect with sharp, sudden shifts in European migration policy. Ultimately, those within the Dutch asylum system lose not only a geographical homeland but also their sense of self. Exile erodes it. A creative director becomes a delivery driver, a pharmacist a construction worker, and a filmmaker an anonymous case number on an immigration officer’s desk.

For Suhail and Sama, this limbo slowly consumes their inner lives. In a reception facility in The Hague, Suhail relies on forgetfulness to dull the ache of legal exclusion. In Rotterdam, Sama fills her notebook with memories of the light they once shared in Sana’a and Cairo. Their twelve-year relationship has survived war, family rejection, displacement, and separation across the Middle East. Now it faces its hardest test: the slow violence of bureaucratic waiting.

Within these camps of transit and precarity, their relationship has become their last archive of belonging, and their longing to be together has become a reality. As Sama says, “I need a house I can call home.”
